01 · the problem

A computer sees letters.
Nothing more.

To a computer, the word dog is the number 100, then 111, then 103 — the codes for the letters d, o, g. That's it. No fur. No tail. No memory of a bark.

So how on earth does it figure out that dog is closer in meaning to puppy than it is to bicycle?

The answer is surprisingly geometric. The model builds a map, and every word gets a spot on it. Words that mean similar things end up near each other. Words that mean different things end up far apart.

The rest of this field guide is about how that map gets made, and why it's the secret that powers nearly everything a language model does.

meaning, it turns out, has a shape.
02 · step one · breaking text into tokens

First, chop the sentence up.

Before the model can place words on its map, it has to decide what counts as a "word." The answer is awkward: not quite words, not quite letters. These in-between pieces are called tokens.

Common words are usually one token. Rare words get chopped into several. Try it — type anything:

words go in. tokens come out. we're just one step into the pipeline.
03 · step two · every token becomes a list of numbers

Now each token gets a vector.

The model looks up the token's ID in a huge table. Out comes a long list of numbers — hundreds, sometimes thousands, long. This list is the token's vector, or embedding.

Think of it like GPS coordinates. On Earth, you need two numbers — latitude and longitude — to pin down a location. In the world of meaning, you need hundreds.

Each number is like one coordinate. It positions the word along some dimension — some quality — that the model has learned is useful for telling meanings apart.

Each column is one dimension the model learned. Warm bars point one way, cool bars the other. Notice: dog and puppy have nearly identical patterns. Cat is close. Bicycle is the opposite of all three.

04 · step three · the map of meaning

Hundreds of numbers become a place.

Here's the big idea. Those vectors aren't random. When you take all the words in the language and plot them using their vectors, they don't scatter evenly — they cluster. Words about food end up in one neighborhood. Animals, another. Emotions, a third.

Real language models use hundreds of dimensions, which is impossible to draw. But if we squash everything down to just three — like compressing a globe into a snow globe you can spin in your hands — the shape is still there. Drag to orbit the map below. Click any word to see its nearest neighbors:

05 · what do the dimensions mean?

Each number tracks one kind of meaning.

Here's the beautiful part. The model isn't told what the dimensions should mean. It figures them out on its own, from seeing billions of sentences.

And when you look at what it came up with, the dimensions often correspond to real qualities — things a human would also pay attention to. Size. Gender. Temperature. Age. Formality. How positive or negative something feels.

Pick an axis below. Watch the same words rearrange themselves along a completely different meaning:

In a real model, there are hundreds of these axes, layered together. A single word gets a position along all of them at once. That's how "queen" can be close to "king" along the royalty dimension while also being close to "woman" along the gender dimension — no contradiction. Space is just that big.

06 · the magic trick

Relationships become directions.

Once words live on a map, the relationships between them become directions you can travel.

The step that takes you from France to Paris is about the same step that takes you from Italy to Rome. Same direction. Same distance. The model learned "capital of" as a little arrow you can add or subtract.

This lets you do arithmetic with meanings, which sounds impossible but works:

paris − france + italy ≈ rome

07 · from words to sentences

Then the words start talking to each other.

Individual word vectors are only the starting point. A sentence isn't a bag of words — order matters, context matters. The word bank in "river bank" sits in a different place than bank in "bank account."

Modern language models solve this with attention. Every token looks at every other token in the sentence and decides how much each one should influence its meaning. Those influences pull each word's vector into a new, context-aware position.

Click a word below to see which others it would "pay attention to" — the thicker the line, the stronger the pull:

After one round of this, every word's vector has shifted to reflect what's around it. After another, it shifts again, incorporating wider context. After dozens of rounds — stacked layers of attention — the whole sentence has been blended into a single unified understanding.

At that point the model can do anything: answer a question, finish the thought, translate it, summarize it. The geometry has done the heavy lifting.

And that's the whole thing.

Break text into tokens. Turn each token into a vector of numbers. Use the vectors to place words on a vast map where nearness means similarity of meaning. Let context pull the vectors around. Read the result.

There's no understanding inside, in the human sense. No pictures, no memories, no felt sense of what dog is. Just geometry — remarkably rich, learned-from-the-shape-of-all-our-writing geometry. And somehow, it's enough.
