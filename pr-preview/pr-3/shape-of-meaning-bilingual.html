the shape of meaning

How language models turn words into points in space — and why that turns out to be almost everything.

Fig 01 · Tokenizer

First, chop the sentence up.

Before the model can place words on its map, it has to decide what counts as a "word." The answer is awkward: not quite words, not quite letters. These in-between pieces are called tokens.

Common words are usually one token. Rare words get chopped into several. Try it — type anything:

whole word word piece ending other
Fig 02 · Tokens become IDs

Each token gets an ID.

Each token has an ID — a number between 0 and about 100,000. The number itself means nothing yet. It's just a label for this particular chunk of text. From here on, the model works with numbers, never letters.

Fig 03 · A map of meaning

Hundreds of numbers become a place.

The model looks up each token's ID in a huge table. Out comes a long list of numbers — hundreds, sometimes thousands, long. This list is the token's vector, or embedding. Think of it like GPS coordinates: on Earth you need two numbers to pin down a location; in the world of meaning, you need hundreds.

Those vectors aren't random. When you plot all the words using their vectors, they cluster — food in one neighborhood, animals in another, emotions in a third. Real models use hundreds of dimensions, which is impossible to draw. Here's a flat, two-dimensional version so you can see the shape:

Fig 04 · One dimension at a time

Each number tracks one kind of meaning.

Here's the beautiful part. The model isn't told what the dimensions should mean. It figures them out on its own, from seeing billions of sentences. The dimensions often correspond to real qualities — size, gender, temperature, age, formality. Pick a word and see how it sits along a few sample dimensions:

In a real model there are hundreds of these axes, layered together. A single word gets a position along all of them at once. That's how "queen" can be close to "king" along the royalty dimension while also being close to "woman" along the gender dimension — no contradiction. Space is just that big.

Fig 05 · The magic trick

Relationships become directions.

Once words live on a map, the relationships between them become directions you can travel. The step that takes you from France to Paris is about the same step that takes you from Italy to Rome. Same direction. Same distance. The model learned "capital of" as a little arrow you can add or subtract. This lets you do arithmetic with meanings, which sounds impossible but works:

king − man + woman = queen

Take "king," subtract the meaning of "man," add the meaning of "woman" — you land on "queen." Nobody told the model that "royalty plus gender" was a direction. It learned it from reading billions of sentences, and the geometry just lined up.

Models also differ in how many tokens they recognize (their vocabulary — 32K to 128K unique tokens) and how much text they can read at once (their context window — from 1K tokens in early GPT-2 up to 10 million in Llama 4 Scout).

Fig 07 · Putting it together

And that's the whole thing.

Break text into tokens. Turn each token into a vector of numbers. Use the vectors to place words on a vast map where nearness means similarity of meaning. Read the result.

There's no understanding inside, in the human sense. No pictures, no memories, no felt sense of what dog is. Just geometry — remarkably rich, learned-from-the-shape-of-all-our-writing geometry. And somehow, it's enough.
